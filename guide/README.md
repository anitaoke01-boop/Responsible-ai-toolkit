# Responsible AI Field Guide (2025)

*A practical review guide for professionals checking work created with AI assistance.*

> **Illustrative guide:** examples and sample data here are synthetic. This guide offers general review practices, not legal, medical, financial, security, or organizational policy advice. Check applicable workplace rules and current authoritative sources before relying on factual or policy claims.

## 1. Use AI where it helps

AI tools can help with a first draft, a plain-language rewrite, a summary to compare with a source, a list of questions, or a repetitive transformation of non-sensitive information. They are most useful when a person can describe the task, provide appropriate source material, and check the result.

Pause before using AI when the task affects a person’s rights, safety, job, money, access to services, or reputation. Do not let an AI output make a consequential decision on its own. Get the right human expertise and follow the rules for the work.

Do not use a tool just because it is available. Ask:

- Is AI allowed for this task under our policies, contract, and applicable rules?
- Can I use a tool approved for this information and purpose?
- Can I check the result against reliable evidence?
- Is there a clear person accountable for the final work?

If any answer is no or unclear, pause and ask the responsible manager, privacy, security, legal, or subject-matter contact. Do not paste material into an unapproved tool to find out whether it is allowed.

## 2. Keep sensitive information out

Treat prompts and uploaded files as information shared with a service. The service may store, process, or expose them according to its configuration and terms. Use only tools approved for the data classification and task.

Never enter secrets or credentials: passwords, access tokens, private keys, recovery codes, or authentication links. Do not paste confidential business plans, unreleased results, customer or employee records, health or financial details, private correspondence, or identifying information into a tool unless your organization has explicitly approved that tool and use.

Use synthetic examples or approved, de-identified data when possible. Removing a name may not be enough: combinations of dates, roles, locations, and unusual events can identify someone. Do not assume a prompt is private because it is in a work account.

Before sharing a file, check the whole file, including comments, hidden sheets, speaker notes, tracked changes, metadata, and attachments. If sensitive content was entered accidentally, follow your organization’s incident-reporting process promptly; do not try to conceal it.

## 3. Verify sources and figures

AI can produce a polished citation that does not exist, misstate a real source, or attach a true quote to the wrong author. Open every important source yourself. Confirm the title, author or issuing body, date, link, relevant passage, and whether the source actually supports the claim. Prefer primary documents and authoritative sources suited to the question.

For each important factual statement, ask: “What evidence would let another reviewer reproduce this?” Keep a source trail in the work product. A citation is a pointer to evidence, not proof that the statement is accurate.

Check figures from their origin. Confirm units, currency, time period, population, denominator, rounding, and whether the figure is a count, rate, estimate, or percentage. Recalculate important arithmetic independently, preferably with a spreadsheet or calculator. Compare totals and subtotals; look for duplicated rows, missing records, and changed filters.

For a chart or table, trace a sample of values back to the source data. Make sure labels and axes describe what the numbers measure. Do not compare unlike periods, groups, or definitions without explaining the difference.

## 4. Look for errors and bias

Read the output as a reviewer, not as an editor polishing a draft. Fluent writing can hide invented details, faulty reasoning, missing caveats, or an answer to a different question. Compare it with the request and the source material line by line when stakes warrant.

Watch for:

- names, dates, quotes, cases, or citations that cannot be found in the source;
- claims that go beyond the evidence or turn a possibility into a certainty;
- calculations with wrong units, denominators, ranges, or assumptions;
- missing exceptions, disagreement, context, or inconvenient evidence;
- stereotypes or different standards applied to people or groups;
- recommendations that treat correlation as cause or overlook who bears the risk.

Bias can enter through the data, the task framing, the tool, or the reviewer’s assumptions. Ask whose perspective is missing, whether the categories fit the people involved, and whether the same evidence would be judged the same way for another person or group. Where appropriate, have a qualified colleague review the potential impact. Do not claim a system is fair based on a single example or a small informal check.

## 5. Keep human review meaningful

The named reviewer owns the final work. Review the original source, the prompt or task, and the AI output. Do not approve a result just because it sounds confident, came from a familiar tool, or passed a quick read.

Match review effort to impact. A low-risk internal wording suggestion may need a brief check. A public statement, safety instruction, personnel recommendation, or decision affecting an individual needs stronger evidence, appropriate expertise, and any required approval. Follow established escalation and recordkeeping rules.

Make the review visible. Record important sources, assumptions, changes, unresolved questions, and who approved the result where your process requires it. If AI use must be disclosed, label it clearly. Do not imply that a person personally performed research or analysis that was not done.

If the output cannot be checked, do not present it as verified. Remove unsupported material, return to the source, seek expertise, or stop the task.

## 6. Treat uncertainty honestly

AI-generated confidence is not evidence. Ask what is known, what is inferred, what is missing, and what could change the conclusion. Separate facts from estimates, assumptions, and recommendations.

Use calibrated wording. Say “the source reports…” when describing a source, “the available records suggest…” when evidence is incomplete, and “I could not verify…” when a check failed. Include a date for time-sensitive information and say when a current authoritative check is still needed.

Do not fill gaps with plausible detail. If sources conflict, describe the disagreement and identify which source is more authoritative for the question. If there is no adequate evidence, say so and explain what evidence would be needed. Escalate uncertainty when the consequences of being wrong are material.

## Review in five moves

1. **Check permission:** task, tool, and data use are allowed.
2. **Protect information:** remove secrets and sensitive details.
3. **Trace claims:** open sources and reproduce important figures.
4. **Challenge the answer:** look for omissions, errors, bias, and unsupported certainty.
5. **Own the decision:** correct, qualify, escalate, or reject before approving.

## Further reading and claims to verify

This guide intentionally avoids asserting specific laws, regulatory duties, or product data-retention behavior. Those vary and can change. Verify current organizational policy and applicable requirements with the appropriate authoritative source before use. Any local adaptation should be reviewed by the organization’s policy owner.
